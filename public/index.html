Hear NASA's views of Earth.

Each track turns a NASA visualization into sound: city lights, the air we breathe, the ocean's great currents. A line crosses the picture, and whatever it crosses plays.

Start here

  1. Choose a track in the list below, with the arrow keys.
  2. Press Tab to reach the player's keyboard area, then Space to play or pause.
  3. In the player, press H to hear every key, and W to ask where you are.
  4. Use headphones if you can: the north sounds in your left ear, the south in your right. On the sea temperature track the line moves from north to south, so there west is left and east is right.
  5. Every key you press is named out loud, with a soft click. Turn that off in Settings.

      How it was made, and why

      Listening comes first

      Most data sonification is made for sighted people, with sound added on top of a chart. We built it the other way round: a blind listener can open this page alone, with no one explaining it. Every key says what it did, every sound has an example button, and W always tells you where you are. The globe and the video are there for sighted friends listening along.

      Reading NASA's pictures

      No numbers are invented. Our Python scripts read NASA's own videos frame by frame. Night lights: brightness, calibrated against ten cities. Air quality: NASA shows four pollutants in strips that slide across one map; we find each strip in every frame and rebuild a whole world map for each pollutant from its colour scale. Ocean: NASA's world map of surface currents, averaged over a year, where brighter flow lines mean faster water; the guided lesson measures how NASA's flow lines move off Florida. El Niño: every daily sea temperature map is read back into degrees from NASA's own colour bar and averaged by month.

      Turning numbers into sound

      The rule is the same on every track, so you only learn it once. Up is higher pitch and your left ear; down is lower and your right. A note rings where a place is bright, thick or fast; a deep drone carries everything under the line. Notes use a pentatonic scale, so they never clash by accident; when they do clash, it means something. One track turns the line on its side: on Warm and cold seas it moves from the Arctic down to Antarctica, so west is your left ear and east your right, and the warmer the sea under it, the more and higher voices join a warm chord.

      Beauty and urgency

      We let the sound carry the feeling of the data. Clean air is a calm, open drone; where pollution is worst, the drone turns sour and the notes slip into a minor key. The ocean gets a harp and no sourness at all: its currents link every coast on Earth. Light that is new since 2012 rises; light that went out falls, on a cello.

      About the data

        Values are read from the colours of NASA's visualizations, so they are relative levels, not satellite units. Not an official NASA product.
